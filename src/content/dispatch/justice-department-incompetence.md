## When Incompetence Serves Power

The Department of Justice's recent failure to redact the names of Jeffrey Epstein's victims is not a routine clerical lapse. It is a signal—one of those rare moments when the facade slips and the machinery of power shows its gears.

## The Pattern of Convenient Errors

We are asked to believe that among the most sensitive documents in contemporary American jurisprudence—materials touching on intelligence connections, elite criminal networks, and the systematic abuse of minors—basic procedures were simply... forgotten.

Consider the probabilities:

- **Redaction software** is standard issue in federal prosecution
- **Review protocols** for victim-sensitive materials are drilled into DOJ personnel
- **Oversight mechanisms** exist precisely to prevent such disclosures

And yet, here we are.

## Reading the Tea Leaves

There are few human organizations less prone to accidental disclosure than federal prosecutors. The timing, the specificity, the subsequent framing—these suggest not error but intention, deployed strategically to create specific outcomes.

## The Question Not Being Asked

Rather than asking *how* the disclosure happened, we might more profitably ask: *Who benefits?* And: *What information remains sealed while this particular release dominates the narrative?*

The victims deserved protection. That protection was deliberately removed. The reasons why tell us more about power in America than the contents of the unsealed documents themselves.
