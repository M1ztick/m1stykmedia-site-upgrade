## The American Dream, Quantified

The notion that anyone can become President is a compelling American myth. The idea that a small-town teacher or community organizer might ascend to the highest office through sheer merit and democratic will is the bedtime story we tell ourselves about democracy.

The reality, as evidenced by campaign finance data, network analysis, and the increasingly explicit role of dark money in electoral politics, is far more stratified.

## The Cost of Entry

Consider the thresholds:

- **Presidential campaigns** now routinely exceed $1 billion in spending
- **Senate races** in competitive states demand $50-100 million
- **House districts** with genuine competition require $5-10 million minimum

These aren't amounts raised by grassroots donations from working-class constituents. These are the buy-in costs for access to a rigged game where the house always wins.

## The Myth of Representation

When we examine policy outcomes against public polling on those same issues, a consistent pattern emerges: elite preferences predict legislative outcomes; mass preferences do not. The correlation between donor interests and policy results is robust; the correlation between public opinion and policy results, particularly on economic matters, is negligible.

This isn't a malfunction of democracy. This *is* the system functioning as designed.

## Reframing the Question

The relevant inquiry isn't "Can anyone become President?" 

The relevant inquiry is: "Given the structural barriers to entry, the cost of campaigning, and the post-election obligations to donors, why would anyone who actually represents majority interests even attempt the climb?"

The silence that follows is the answer.
