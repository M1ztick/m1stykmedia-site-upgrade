# How the CIA Was Born: From OSS to Cold War Shadow War

## Spies for a World War

Long before the Central Intelligence Agency existed, the United States fought World War II with a hybrid intelligence and special-operations service called the **Office of Strategic Services (OSS)**. Created in 1942 and led by **William J. "Wild Bill" Donovan**, the OSS ran espionage, sabotage, propaganda, and guerrilla warfare behind enemy lines.

Its genealogy is straightforward:

**Office of the Coordinator of Information (COI, 1941) → Office of Strategic Services (OSS, 1942–1945) → Strategic Services Unit (SSU, 1945) → Central Intelligence Group / National Intelligence Authority (1946) → Central Intelligence Agency (1947).**

The CIA was, in every meaningful way, the peacetime child of the OSS.

## Truman Kills the OSS — Then Realizes He Needs It

On **September 20, 1945**, just weeks after Japan's surrender, **President Harry S. Truman** signed **Executive Order 9621** dissolving the OSS. The order gave the agency only ten days to shut down.

Truman distrusted Donovan and worried about creating a permanent American "secret police." The OSS intelligence branches were split up: the **Research and Analysis Branch** went to the State Department (later becoming the Bureau of Intelligence and Research), while the **Secret Intelligence (SI)** and **Counter-Espionage (X-2)** branches went to the War Department under a temporary **Strategic Services Unit (SSU)**.

Donovan fought back. In August 1945 he sent Truman a memorandum arguing for a centralized peacetime intelligence agency with both analytic and covert capabilities. Truman ignored him.

But by early 1946, Soviet behavior in Eastern Europe convinced Truman that the United States still needed espionage and intelligence coordination.

## The Central Intelligence Group (1946)

On **January 22, 1946**, Truman issued a presidential directive creating two bodies:

- The **National Intelligence Authority (NIA)** — a supervisory panel of cabinet secretaries and a presidential representative.
- The **Central Intelligence Group (CIG)** — the operational agency, led by a **Director of Central Intelligence (DCI)**.

The NIA oversaw the CIG. The CIG got all its money and people from the State, War, and Navy Departments. It could coordinate and analyze intelligence, but it had no independent budget, no independent legal charter, and no clear authority to conduct covert operations.

**Rear Admiral Sidney Souers** became the first DCI. Donovan reportedly sneered that the CIG was little more than a **"debating society."** He was soon proved right.

## The National Security Act of 1947

As Cold War tensions hardened, Truman and Congress agreed that the United States needed a stronger, independent intelligence agency — one that could both coordinate the sprawling military and diplomatic intelligence apparatus and carry out the secret work of containment.

Truman signed the **National Security Act on July 26, 1947**. It took effect on **September 18, 1947**. The Act created:

- The **Central Intelligence Agency (CIA)**
- The **National Security Council (NSC)**
- The office of the **Secretary of Defense** and a more unified military establishment

The Act gave the CIA a deliberately broad mandate. It was to coordinate the nation's intelligence activities and to "correlate, evaluate, and disseminate" intelligence affecting national security. But it also authorized the Agency to perform **"such other functions and duties related to intelligence affecting the national security as the National Security Council may from time to time direct."**

That single catch-all sentence became the legal foundation for covert action, paramilitary operations, and regime change for decades.

**Roscoe H. Hillenkoetter**, the CIG's former deputy director, became the first DCI. Roughly **one-third of the CIA's early staff were OSS veterans**.

## Eisenhower: Not Abolition, But Expansion and Oversight

Contrary to the idea that Eisenhower tried to dissolve the CIA, the Eisenhower years (1953–1961) were the period in which the Agency took on its modern shape and scale. Eisenhower valued covert action as a cheaper, deniable alternative to direct military intervention.

### Under Eisenhower:

- **1953**: The CIA and Britain's MI6 overthrew Iranian Prime Minister **Mohammad Mossadegh** in **Operation Ajax**, restoring the Shah.
- **1954**: The CIA overthrew Guatemalan President **Jacobo Árbenz** in **Operation PBSuccess**, triggering decades of instability and civil war.
- **1954**: NSC 5412 formalized CIA authority over covert action; **NSC 5412/2 (1955)** set review procedures for clandestine operations.
- **1955**: The **Doolittle Report**, commissioned by Eisenhower, advised that the United States had to stop worrying about "fair play" in intelligence because the Soviet Union did not.
- **1956**: Eisenhower created the **President's Board of Consultants on Foreign Intelligence Activities (PBCFIA)** — an outside advisory body of prominent civilians, including **Joseph P. Kennedy**, meant to give the president candid, independent assessments of U.S. intelligence. It later became the **President's Foreign Intelligence Advisory Board (PFIAB)**.
- **1956**: The same board produced the **Bruce-Lovett Report**, which warned that CIA covert operations were expanding beyond meaningful oversight.

Eisenhower did not try to abolish the CIA. He expanded its role and then tried to impose **external oversight** on it.

## The Built-In Contradiction

From birth, the CIA was caught between two missions: **central intelligence coordinator** and **covert-action instrument**. The National Security Act explicitly authorized the first. The Cold War demanded the second.

That open-ended mandate allowed the Agency to become a global instrument of secret intervention — for better or worse. It also planted the seeds of future scandal.

## Early Controversies

| Event | Year | What Happened |
|---|---|---|
| **Operation Ajax — Iran** | 1953 | CIA/MI6 coup to reverse oil nationalization; restored Shah Mohammad Reza Pahlavi. Long-term consequences include the 1979 Islamic Revolution. |
| **Operation PBSuccess — Guatemala** | 1954 | CIA-backed coup against Jacobo Árbenz; led to military dictatorships and a 36-year civil war. |
| **Project MKUltra** | 1953–1973 | Illegal mind-control and interrogation experiments on unwitting subjects using LSD, hypnosis, electroshock, and sensory deprivation. Most records were destroyed in 1973. |
| **ZR/RIFLE** | 1960s | CIA program to develop a generalized assassination capability, later exposed by the Church Committee. |
| **Bay of Pigs Invasion** | 1961 | Failed CIA-backed exile invasion of Cuba; humiliated the Kennedy administration and led to resignations of DCI **Allen Dulles** and planner **Richard Bissell**. |
| **Family Jewels Report** | 1973–1975 | Internal CIA inventory of illegal or questionable activities; leaked in 1975, prompting congressional investigation. |
| **Church Committee** | 1975–1976 | Senate inquiry that exposed assassination plots, COINTELPRO collaboration, MKUltra, and other abuses; led to permanent congressional intelligence oversight committees. |

## Verdict

The CIA was not created in secret by a single cabal, nor was it a Cold War afterthought. It emerged from a clear wartime need, was briefly dismantled, and then was rebuilt — fast — because the emergence of Soviet power made centralized intelligence and covert action seem indispensable.

Its birth certificate is public law: the **National Security Act of 1947**. But the scope of what that law authorized — especially its vague "other functions" clause — remains the subject of legitimate historical and political debate to this day.
