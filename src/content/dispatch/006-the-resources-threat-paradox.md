# The Resources/Threat Paradox: What the "FLT" Designation Is Really About

You've probably seen the acronym by now: **FLT**, shorthand for "far-left terrorism." It is a word-weapon, like "WMD" before it, deployed to make dissent sound dangerous and political opposition sound illegal.

On July 16, 2026, the Trump administration convened a State Department ministerial on what it called the "resurgence of political terrorism." Secretary of State Marco Rubio told representatives from roughly 60 countries that the United States would begin treating far-left extremism "with the same seriousness and ferocity the world has long reserved for jihadist terrorism." President Trump had already signed National Security Presidential Memorandum 7 (NSPM-7), directing federal agencies to "disrupt, identify, defund, debank, arrest, and prosecute" domestic left-wing "political terrorists."

But here is the uncomfortable question the administration refuses to answer honestly: **If the goal is to protect Americans from political violence, why is the government pouring counterterrorism resources into a threat that the data shows is smaller, spikier, and less lethal than the far-right violence it has simultaneously pardoned?**

## What the numbers actually say

Political violence by the far left is real. In 2025, after years of near-invisibility, it ticked upward sharply. A study by the Center for Strategic and International Studies (CSIS) found that by Independence Day 2025, left-wing terrorist attacks and plots outnumbered far-right ones for the first time in more than 30 years: **five left-wing incidents, one right-wing incident**. The report noted that 2025 was on pace to be "the left's most violent year in more than three decades."

That is a legitimate data point. It is not, however, the whole picture.

Over the past decade, CSIS data shows that **left-wing attacks killed 13 people**, while **right-wing attacks killed 112**. Since 2001, far-right extremist violence has accounted for an estimated **75 to 80 percent of U.S. domestic terrorism deaths**. From 2022 through 2024, a separate analysis found that **every single one of 61 political killings in the United States was committed by right-wing extremists**.

The 2025 uptick is a deviation, not a reversal. The CSIS researchers themselves cautioned that the numbers should not be used as "an excuse for a crackdown on legitimate organizations."

Yet the administration's 2026 counterterrorism strategy identifies only three primary threats: Islamist terrorism, narco-terrorism, and "violent left-wing extremists, including anarchists and anti-fascists." Far-right extremism is not treated as a comparable priority.

## The double standard in action

The asymmetry is not merely rhetorical. On January 20, 2025, President Trump issued "full, complete, and unconditional" pardons or commutations to nearly everyone charged in the January 6, 2021 assault on the U.S. Capitol. Those defendants included people convicted of beating police with flagpoles, fire extinguishers, and baseball bats; spraying officers with bear spray; and plotting seditious conspiracy. Some had prior convictions for rape, domestic violence, and sexual abuse of minors. According to the House Judiciary Committee, at least ten pardoned Jan. 6 defendants have since been rearrested for new crimes.

Compare that to the nine anti-ICE protesters tried after a demonstration at the Prairieland Detention Center in Texas, where a police officer was shot in the neck and survived. Although the defendants denied being members of Antifa and were linked by prosecutors partly through an Emma Goldman reading group, five received sentences of **30 to 100 years** in prison—**longer than any sentence handed down to a convicted Jan. 6 rioter**, all of whom were later pardoned.

When violence is committed in the name of the administration's base, the offenders are forgiven. When violence—or even proximity to protest—can be attributed to the left, the state deploys its heaviest machinery.

## Why Antifa?

The administration's signature target is Antifa. In 2025, Trump designated Antifa a "domestic terrorist organization." The State Department has since designated four European antifascist groups as Foreign Terrorist Organizations, with more promised.

The problem, as noted even by Trump's own former FBI director Christopher Wray and the nonpartisan Congressional Research Service, is that **Antifa is not an organization**. It has no leader, no headquarters, no membership roster, no command structure. It is a loose anti-fascist ideology adopted by autonomous local groups and individuals.

So why designate it?

Because the vagueness is the point. A "terrorist" label with no membership criteria can be stretched to fit protesters, donors, journalists, lawyers, nonprofits, and anyone whose politics the state finds inconvenient. Under material-support laws and financial sanctions, a legal defense fund becomes terrorism financing; protest safety training becomes terrorist instruction; research on far-right movements becomes terrorist propaganda; a broken window becomes "economic sabotage."

The European Commission confirmed this in 2022, telling members of Parliament that Antifa cannot be prosecuted as a terrorist group under EU law because it is "not an organisation but the collective name used by various autonomous and often informal groups." In the United States, Congress has never created a domestic terrorist designation regime precisely because it would implicate protected speech and association.

The administration is getting around that firewall by using executive orders, NSPM-7, foreign designations, and state-level copycat laws to build a parallel system anyway.

## The chilling effect is intentional

NSPM-7 does not limit itself to violence. It flags ideological orientations—"anti-Americanism," "anti-capitalism," "anti-Christianity"—as indicators of domestic terrorism. It defines terrorist-supporting conduct to include "organized doxing campaigns, swatting, rioting, looting, trespass, assault, destruction of property, threats of violence, and civil disorder."

Some of those acts are genuinely criminal. But bundled together under the label of terrorism, and paired with ideological markers of belief, the framework criminalizes not just conduct but association and opinion. As Lawfare noted, when government surveillance turns inward "it is rarely confined to its stated purpose"—a history stretching from the FBI's COINTELPRO harassment of Martin Luther King Jr. to the post-9/11 surveillance of Muslim communities and antiwar activists.

The ACLU told Reuters that the new designations "could be used to target lawful protest activity and political opponents rather than genuine security threats." Multiple anonymous U.S. officials told the Washington Post they worry the administration wants to "unlock powerful counterterrorism tools" against American activists.

This is the pattern: redefine political opposition as terrorism, then use terrorism's exceptional legal powers against it.

## Who benefits?

The answer is structural. Authoritarian movements rarely begin by criminalizing everyone at once. They begin with groups that are easy to vilify and hard to defend in polite company: anarchists, anti-fascists, campus protesters, immigration activists. Once the legal machinery is built, the category of "terrorist" can expand to include trade unionists, civil rights lawyers, journalists, opposition parties, and ordinary citizens.

The language of "FLT" is not primarily a counterterrorism tool. It is a political sorting mechanism. It tells Americans which violence is patriotic and which is terror, which protesters are citizens and which are enemies, which opinions are protected and which are suspicious.

The data says the far right has been responsible for the bulk of American political deaths. The policy says the far left is the existential threat. That gap between reality and rhetoric is the story.
