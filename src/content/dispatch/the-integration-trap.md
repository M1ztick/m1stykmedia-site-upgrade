# The Integration Trap

## How Congress Is Binding U.S. Military Architecture to a State Under International Indictment

On June 4, 2026, the House Armed Services Committee advanced a provision that received minimal media attention and almost no public debate. Buried on page 127 of a 505-page draft bill, Section 224—the "United States-Israel Defense Technology Cooperation Initiative"—proposes to do something unprecedented in the history of American foreign military relationships: structurally fuse the U.S. defense industrial base with that of a foreign state whose leadership is currently under international criminal indictment for war crimes and crimes against humanity.

The measure is not merely about sharing technology. It is about *integration*—creating permanent bureaucratic pipelines, co-production agreements, licensing frameworks, and joint research and development programs that would make Israeli-origin systems functionally inseparable from American military architecture. The provision establishes a Pentagon "executive agent" whose sole purpose is to facilitate this entanglement across domains including artificial intelligence, autonomous systems, quantum computing, cyber warfare, counter-drone technology, and directed energy weapons.

What it does not establish—what it conspicuously omits—are any mechanisms for accountability.

---

## The Legislative Architecture

Section 224 did not emerge spontaneously. Its origins reveal a deliberate lobbying strategy designed to advance an agenda that could not survive standalone public scrutiny.

In February 2026, Representative Don Davis (D-NC) and Representative Ronny Jackson (R-TX) introduced the standalone "U.S.-Israel FUTURES Act"—the Framework for Upgraded Technologies, Unified Research, and Enhanced Security. Senators Ted Budd (R-NC) and Kirsten Gillibrand (D-NY) introduced companion legislation in the Senate. Both bills stalled in committee. They attracted too much attention.

So the same legislative architecture—much of the identical language—was transplanted into the National Defense Authorization Act, where it benefits from the annual bill's must-pass status and voluminous page count. The NDAA has passed Congress every year for six decades. Amendments receive minimal debate. The public does not read 505-page defense bills.

The maneuver worked. On June 4, the House Armed Services Committee advanced the mark with bipartisan support from Chairman Mike Rogers (R-AL) and Ranking Member Adam Smith (D-WA). The bill now proceeds to full House consideration, then Senate markup, then conference committee—each stage offering opportunities for expansion but few for contraction.

Josh Paul, the former State Department official who resigned in protest over U.S. Gaza policy, summarized the strategy accurately: "What Congress is trying to do now is find different ways of entrenching the relationship so deep in America's own defense industrial base that it's impossible to root it out."

## The Corporate Beneficiaries

The technologies specified in Section 224—AI targeting systems, autonomous weapons platforms, quantum encryption, directed energy—are not abstract capabilities. They are proprietary products developed by a small cluster of Israeli defense contractors with extensive U.S. market presence and lobbying operations.

### Rafael Advanced Defense Systems
Rafael is the primary developer of **Iron Beam**, the high-energy laser system that Israel fielded operationally in late 2025—making it the first nation to deploy directed energy weapons outside test environments. Rafael also manufactures **David's Sling** (medium-range missile defense, co-developed with the U.S. Missile Defense Agency), **Trophy** active protection systems (selected by four NATO countries for Leopard 2 A8 tank fleets), and the **SPIKE** family of precision-guided munitions.

Rafael operates **Rafael USA**, its American subsidiary, and in early 2026 established a joint venture with **Kratos Defense** called "Prometheus, Propulsion & Explosive Systems"—explicitly designed to penetrate U.S. procurement channels. The company has also partnered with **Raytheon** on multiple programs.

### Israel Aerospace Industries (IAI)
IAI is the prime contractor for the **Arrow** missile family—exoatmospheric interceptors co-developed with the U.S. since the 1980s. In December 2025, IAI signed a **$6.5 billion contract** with Germany for Arrow 3 systems—the largest defense export deal in Israeli history. The company is now developing **Arrow 4** with the U.S. Missile Defense Agency for deliveries after 2030.

IAI's **Elta** subsidiary builds radar systems for David's Sling and other integrated air defense architectures. The company has structured its operations to ensure that U.S. missile defense programs depend on Israeli sensor technology.

### Elbit Systems
Elbit manufactures command-and-control systems for Arrow 3, develops competing high-power laser systems to Rafael's Iron Beam, and produces the **Hermes** family of armed drones used extensively in Gaza, Lebanon, and the West Bank. Elbit maintains **Elbit Systems of America**, which functions as a domestic entity for lobbying, contracting, and political contribution purposes.

These three companies—Rafael, IAI, and Elbit—constitute the core of Israel's defense export capacity. They also constitute the core beneficiaries of Section 224's integration framework. And they have spent years building the political infrastructure to ensure Congress delivers.

## The Lobbying Apparatus

The path from stalled standalone bill to buried NDAA provision runs directly through Washington's most influential foreign-policy lobbying organizations.

### AIPAC
On February 19, 2026—the same day the FUTURES Act was introduced—the American Israel Public Affairs Committee **publicly endorsed** the legislation, highlighting provisions "encouraging US-based co-production, joint ventures, and manufacturing partnerships with Israeli industry." AIPAC's first-quarter lobbying disclosure, filed with the Senate, explicitly reports lobbying on the U.S.-Israel FUTURES Act at both Capitol Hill and the Department of Defense.

AIPAC's campaign finance operation has become increasingly visible in recent years, spending over **$100 million** in the 2024 election cycle to defeat candidates critical of Israeli policy. The organization does not disclose which members receive specific allocations, but its expenditures are public record.

### Foundation for Defense of Democracies (FDD)
The FDD—originally incorporated as "Emet," Hebrew for "truth," with IRS filings committing to "enhancing Israel's image in North America"—published a December 2025 report titled *Beyond the U.S.-Israel MOU: The Case for a Strategic Partnership Agreement*. The report advocated precisely the integration framework that appeared two months later in the FUTURES Act and now in Section 224.

When Representative Davis announced the FUTURES Act, FDD was the **only outside validator** quoted in his press release. Tyler Stapleton, FDD's senior director of government relations, stated: "FDD Action strongly supports the bipartisan United States-Israel Futures Act."

### The Ideological Layer
Behind the lobbying lies a more ambitious vision. David Wurmser—architect of the 1996 "Clean Break" document that advised Netanyahu to abandon Oslo, overthrow Saddam Hussein, and reshape the Middle East—published *Israel 2048: A Blueprint for a Rising Asymmetric Geopolitical Power* in February 2026. The report advocates making Israel "indispensable" to U.S. military architecture as a mechanism for securing American authorization for Israeli "preventative wars" against Iran, Turkey, and potentially Egypt.

This is not conspiracy theory. It is published policy analysis, distributed by the Henry Jackson Society, advocating precisely the structural dependency that Section 224 would create.

## The Accountability Vacuum

Section 224 runs to approximately 1,200 words. It specifies research domains, co-production mechanisms, licensing frameworks, and reporting requirements. It does not contain any of the following:

- **Human rights review** of prospective Israeli technologies
- **Civilian harm audit** requirement for integrated AI targeting systems
- **Conditional suspension** mechanisms if partner state engages in documented violations of international humanitarian law
- **Prohibition** on integrating technologies from firms with documented records of surveillance misuse
- **Transparency requirements** for the executive agent's determinations
- **Congressional notification** before specific technology transfers occur

The sole accountability mechanism is a requirement that the Secretary of Defense submit "regular reports" to Congress. The content of these reports is unspecified. Their classification status is unspecified. Whether they are made public is unspecified.

Compare this to the **Turkey-F-35 precedent**. When Turkey purchased Russia's S-400 air defense system, the Pentagon expelled Ankara from the F-35 program—but spent **years** restructuring supply chains to disentangle Turkish manufacturers from hundreds of component production lines. That was one program, kicked out deliberately. Section 224 would create entanglement across multiple technology domains simultaneously, without any off-ramp mechanism.

As Sarah Harrison of the International Crisis Group noted in comments to *Inside Defense*: "Moving support out of the foreign-aid framework and into Pentagon procurement, licensing, co-production and research channels" makes the relationship far less visible and far harder to condition or terminate.

## The ICC Problem

On November 21, 2024, the International Criminal Court issued arrest warrants for **Benjamin Netanyahu** and former Defense Minister **Yoav Gallant** for alleged war crimes and crimes against humanity in Gaza. The warrants remain active. Netanyahu and Gallant are fugitives from international justice.

Under the Rome Statute, ICC member states are obligated to arrest indicates who enter their territory. Nations including **Switzerland, Belgium, Spain, South Africa, Jordan, Turkey, and Ireland** have affirmed their commitment to execute the warrant. **Hungary**, which hosted Netanyahu in April 2025, refused arrest and was subsequently found by ICC judges to be in non-compliance; the Assembly of States Parties declined to take decisive action in December 2025.

The United States is **not** an ICC member state and has no direct legal obligation to arrest. But Section 224 would create something more structurally problematic than a simple refusal to cooperate: it would make the U.S. military-industrial complex **operationally interdependent** with the military apparatus of a state whose commander-in-chief is internationally indicted for directing crimes against humanity.

The complicity question is not hypothetical. If U.S. personnel operate systems co-developed with Israeli firms, and those systems are employed in operations serving an ICC-indicted command structure, the United States moves from bystander to enabler—not in a legal sense the ICC can adjudicate, but in a political and moral sense that erodes whatever remains of American claims to leadership based on rules-based order.

## The Technologies in Question

Section 224 explicitly names domains where Israeli firms hold proprietary positions and documented records of civilian harm.

### Artificial Intelligence Targeting
In April 2024, *+972 Magazine* and *Local Call* published investigations revealing **"Lavender"** and **"The Gospel" (Habsora)**—AI systems employed by the Israel Defense Forces to generate targeting lists in Gaza.

- **Lavender** reportedly produced a list of **37,000 individuals** classified as Hamas members, with target approval times reduced to approximately 20 seconds. IDF sources admitted that operators treated low-level targets as "garbage" and accepted that "most casualties were women and children."
- **The Gospel** reportedly identified structures rather than individuals, generating hundreds of targets daily with minimal human review.
- **"Where's Daddy?"** software tracked individuals until they entered family residences, then triggered strikes—maximizing casualties among non-combatants.

The IDF's stated "collateral damage threshold" was reportedly **up to 20 civilians per strike**—applied automatically without individual assessment of military necessity, proportionality, or distinction.

These are not defensive systems. They are **offensive targeting architectures** that remove human judgment from life-and-death decisions. Section 224 would facilitate their integration into U.S. systems without audit, review, or prohibition.

### Surveillance Infrastructure
Israeli surveillance systems deployed in Palestinian territories include:
- **Blue Wolf**: Gamified facial recognition; soldiers ranked for capturing Palestinian faces
- **Red Wolf**: Checkpoint facial scanning with color-coded permission status
- **Wolf Pack**: CCTV-based tracking in Hebron, with detained Palestinians held before cameras for database matching
- **Lavender-linked surveillance**: Real-time biometric and behavioral data collection feeding targeting algorithms

The March 2025 investigative confirmation that these systems collected social media activity, phone records, and daily movement patterns for AI targeting represents a merging of **total surveillance and automated lethal response** that no legal framework currently governs.

### Pegasus and NSO Group
In 2021, the U.S. Commerce Department **blacklisted NSO Group**—the Israeli firm behind Pegasus spyware—citing its role in "transnational repression." Pegasus was found on the phones of journalists, activists, opposition politicians, and heads of state across multiple continents.

NSO Group's technology class—zero-click mobile exploitation, geolocation extraction, encrypted communication penetration—is functionally continuous with the "cyber warfare" domain that Section 224 explicitly names for integration. The legislation contains no mechanism to exclude firms with documented records of surveillance abuse. The executive agent has discretion, and discretion in defense procurement typically favors existing relationships over risk assessment.

## The Asymmetry Problem

Proponents of Section 224 frame it as "partnership." But integration without reciprocal accountability is not partnership. It is **clientelism** or **capture**.

| Dimension | U.S.-UK | U.S.-Australia (AUKUS) | **U.S.-Israel (Section 224)** |
|-----------|---------|------------------------|-------------------------------|
| Accountability mechanism | Shared courts, parliamentary inquiry | Congressional oversight in all three states | **None specified** |
| Technology direction | Bidirectional, peer-reviewed | Bidirectional, nuclear submarine tech transfer | **Primarily Israel → U.S.** |
| Human rights conditionality | Implicit in shared legal standards | Explicit in trilateral agreements | **Absent** |
| Civilian harm review | Independent military justice systems | Public reporting requirements | **None** |
| ICC exposure | None (no arrest warrants) | None | **Commander-in-chief indicted** |
| Off-ramp mechanism | NATO suspension procedures | Treaty withdrawal clauses | **None specified** |

The U.S.-Turkey F-35 expulsion demonstrates that disentanglement is possible but costly and time-consuming. Section 224 would create entanglement deliberately, across multiple domains, with no architectural provision for reversal.

## The Democratic Deficit

Sixty percent of Americans now hold an unfavorable view of Israel, according to Pew Research data from April 2026. Among Democrats and Americans under 30, opposition to unconditional military support has reached near-majority levels. The U.S.-Israel relationship has never been less popular.

Section 224 is designed precisely to **circumvent** this democratic shift. By moving support from visible foreign aid (which Congress can debate and the public can understand) into opaque Pentagon procurement channels, the provision makes Israeli military integration technologically irreversible and politically invisible.

The executive agent will not hold public hearings. The technology integration pathways will be classified. The co-production agreements will be subject to FOIA exemptions. And the defense contractors who benefit—Rafael, IAI, Elbit, and their American subsidiaries—will continue making campaign contributions, hiring retired Pentagon officials, and funding think tanks that produce the reports validating the next integration push.

This is not how democracies make decisions about war and peace. It is how **oligopolies** entrench themselves against democratic accountability.

## The Question Before Congress

Section 224 is not yet law. It must clear the full House, survive Senate markup, emerge from conference committee, and receive presidential signature. At each stage, amendments can strip it, modify it, or strengthen it.

Representatives Thomas Massie (R-KY) and Ro Khanna (D-CA) have already signaled opposition. Their challenge will attract support if the provision receives public scrutiny it was designed to avoid.

The relevant question is not whether the U.S. and Israel should cooperate on defense technology. They already do, have for decades, and will continue. The question is whether that cooperation should be **structurally conditioned** on respect for human rights, **audited** for civilian harm, **transparent** to congressional oversight, and **reversible** if the partner state violates international law.

Section 224 answers each of these questions in the negative. It proposes permanent, unconditional, opaque entanglement with a military apparatus currently led by an internationally indicted fugitive, employing AI systems documented to have automated mass killing, and selling surveillance technologies that democratic nations have blacklisted.

The legislation's supporters call this "cooperation."

A more accurate term would be **capture**.
