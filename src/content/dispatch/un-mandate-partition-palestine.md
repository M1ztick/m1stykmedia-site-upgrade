# From Balfour to Nakba: How Palestine Was Stolen by Decree and by Force

## I. A Colonial Frame from the Start

Before the British arrived, Palestine had been part of the Ottoman Empire for roughly 400 years. It was not empty. It was not waiting. Its people—Arab Muslims, Arab Christians, and a small indigenous Jewish minority—lived in cities and villages across a functioning society. Agriculture, trade, religious pilgrimage, and local governance had long shaped the land. The population was overwhelmingly Arab, but it was not monolithic: Bedouin communities, urban merchants, peasant farmers, and minority communities all shared the territory.

During World War I, Britain made three promises that it never intended to keep simultaneously:

- **To the Arabs** (Hussein–McMahon correspondence, 1915–16): support for an independent Arab kingdom if they rebelled against the Ottomans.
- **To the French** (Sykes–Picot Agreement, 1916): a secret carve-up of the Middle East into colonial spheres.
- **To the Zionists** (Balfour Declaration, 1917): support for "the establishment in Palestine of a national home for the Jewish people."

There is no honest reading of these three commitments that makes them compatible. Palestine was promised to its Arab inhabitants, then promised again to European Zionists, while Britain and France divided the region between themselves. The contradictions were not accidental; they were colonial. Britain needed Arab rebellion against the Ottomans, wanted French agreement on the postwar map, and hoped Jewish support would help wartime propaganda and postwar influence.

## II. The Balfour Declaration: A Land Commitment in Someone Else's Country

The Balfour Declaration of November 2, 1917, is often treated in Western discourse as a humanitarian gesture toward Jewish self-determination. But its immediate effect was imperial: a British foreign minister promising a piece of someone else's country to a European political movement.

The declaration supported a "national home for the Jewish people" in Palestine while claiming it should not prejudice the "civil and religious rights of existing non-Jewish communities." The phrasing is itself revealing. The Palestinians were described as "non-Jewish communities" rather than as the majority population of the country. In 1917, Jews made up roughly 5–10% of Palestine's population. Muslims and Christians made up the rest.

This contradiction was never resolved. It was enforced. The League of Nations **Mandate for Palestine (1922/1923)** incorporated the Balfour Declaration and gave Britain legal authority to administer the territory while facilitating Jewish immigration, land acquisition, and the development of Jewish national institutions. The Mandate explicitly required the mandatory power to "secure the establishment of the Jewish national home" and to encourage Jewish immigration. Palestinians, who had sought independence and self-determination, were treated as a demographic problem to be managed.

## III. The Settler Project and the Transformation of Land

Jewish immigration to Palestine began to grow in the late nineteenth century, and it accelerated sharply after 1905 and again following World War I. By 1947, the Jewish population had risen from roughly 56,000 in 1918 to about 600,000—approximately one-third of the total population.

Many immigrants were refugees from Russian pogroms, European antisemitism, and later the Holocaust. Their motives for seeking safety were human and understandable. But their arrival was organized through Zionist institutions—the Jewish National Fund, the Palestine Office, the Jewish Agency—whose purpose was not simply to resettle refugees but to build a Jewish national entity in a land with a non-Jewish majority.

The engine of this transformation was land. Between 1880 and 1947, Zionist agencies acquired property, often from absentee Ottoman or Arab landlords. Under Ottoman and Mandate property law, these transactions were legal. Their social effects were severe. Arab tenant farmers who had cultivated the land for generations faced eviction. Entire villages lost their agricultural base. Zionist settlements frequently barred Arab labor through policies of **Hebrew labor** (Avoda Ivrit), which deepened economic separation. The Zionist labor movement did not seek integration with Palestinian society; it sought the construction of a self-contained Jewish economic and political society that would eventually become a Jewish state.

By 1947, Jews owned roughly 6–7% of the land in Palestine. The population was about two-thirds Arab. Yet the political structure being built—with Jewish National Fund ownership, Jewish-only labor norms, separate schools, health services, and paramilitary organizations—was designed to create the preconditions of statehood.

## IV. The British Get Bitten

Britain created the political conditions for Zionist expansion, then tried to slow it when Arab revolt and regional pressure made continued Jewish immigration politically costly. The **1936–1939 Arab revolt**, a mass uprising against British rule and Zionist land purchases, was brutally suppressed by British forces. By the end of the revolt, roughly 5,000 Palestinians had been killed, thousands wounded, and the Palestinian leadership decapitated through imprisonment, exile, and execution.

The **1939 White Paper** then restricted Jewish immigration and proposed a binational Palestine within ten years. Zionist militias that had grown under British protection now turned on their sponsor.

- The **Haganah**, linked to the Jewish Agency, was the largest Jewish militia. It carried out clandestine immigration operations and eventually coordinated military campaigns with smaller groups.
- The **Irgun Zvai Leumi**, led by Menachem Begin, launched attacks against British personnel, police stations, and markets. In December 1948, Albert Einstein, Hannah Arendt, and other Jewish intellectuals wrote a letter to the *New York Times* condemning Begin's party, Herut, as "a terrorist, right-wing chauvinist organization in Palestine, closely akin in its organization, methods, political philosophy and social appeal to the Nazi and Fascist parties."
- **Lehi** (the Stern Gang), led by Yitzhak Shamir, assassinated British officials and at one point sought support from Fascist Italy and Nazi Germany as potential allies against Britain.

On **July 22, 1946**, Irgun operatives bombed the **King David Hotel** in Jerusalem, the headquarters of British colonial administration. Ninety-one people were killed: 41 Arabs, 28 Britons, 17 Jews, and 5 others. Some warnings were reportedly telephoned, but they were unclear or ignored. The attack destroyed part of the Secretariat and military command. Many historians classify it as terrorism. A plaque at the site today commemorates the bombers as freedom fighters.

The Haganah, Irgun, and Lehi would later form the core of the **Israel Defense Forces**. Two of their commanders—Menachem Begin and Yitzhak Shamir—would later serve as prime ministers of Israel.

## V. The U.N. Vote: A Decision Palestinians Did Not Make

By 1947, Britain was exhausted by the cost and violence of the Mandate. In February 1947 it announced its intention to withdraw and referred the matter to the United Nations. The U.N. Special Committee on Palestine (UNSCOP) toured the region and recommended partition.

On **November 29, 1947**, the U.N. General Assembly adopted **Resolution 181**, calling for:

- A **Jewish state** on about 56% of the land of Mandatory Palestine.
- An **Arab state** on about 42% of the land.
- An **international zone** (corpus separatum) around Jerusalem and Bethlehem.

The plan was presented as a compromise. In material terms, it was not. Jews made up roughly 30–33% of the population and owned about 6–7% of the land. The proposed Jewish state was to receive 56% of the territory, including most of the fertile coastal plain, the eastern Galilee, and the Negev. Within the proposed Jewish state, nearly half the population would have been Arab. The proposed Arab state was fragmented into three non-contiguous sections and lacked a Red Sea port or direct land connection to neighboring Arab states.

Palestinians were not represented at the U.N. deliberations. The Arab Higher Committee, which claimed to speak for the Palestinian population, was not given a seat at the table. The U.N. committee did not consult Palestinian society in any meaningful way. The deliberations were shaped instead by lobbying from Zionist organizations, pressure from the United States (which pushed for partition), and Cold War calculations.

The resolution passed **33–13**, with **10 abstentions**, on November 29, 1947. It required a two-thirds majority.

Zionist leaders accepted the partition plan as a strategic foundation, not a final border. Arab leaders rejected it as the imposition of a settler state on a colonized majority. Both readings had consequences. Only one side had the military organization—and the international supporters—to reshape the outcome.

## VI. From Partition to Conquest: 1947–1949

Violence erupted within hours of the U.N. vote. What began as intercommunal clashes and reprisals quickly became a military campaign to seize territory and clear populations.

Between November 1947 and July 1949, Zionist and then Israeli forces went on the offensive. They moved beyond the U.N. partition lines. They destroyed or depopulated hundreds of Palestinian villages. They carried out massacres, the most infamous of which was at **Deir Yassin** on April 9, 1948, where Irgun and Lehi fighters killed more than 100 Palestinian civilians, including women and children.

The massacre was not hidden. It was publicized, and its effect was intended. Survivors fled. Neighboring villages, hearing the reports, abandoned their homes. The pattern repeated across the country: direct expulsions, whisper campaigns, shelling, and the collapse of Palestinian social order under British withdrawal and Arab military disarray.

By the end of the 1948 war, Israel controlled roughly **78% of Mandatory Palestine**—substantially more than the 56% allocated by Resolution 181. Approximately **750,000 Palestinians were expelled or fled**. They took with them deeds, keys, and the expectation of return. That return was blocked by Israeli soldiers and legislation.

This is the **Nakba**, the Arabic word for catastrophe. It was not a byproduct of war. It was the result of years of planning, superior military organization, the absence of unified Palestinian leadership after the 1936–39 revolt, and international indifference.

## VII. The Architecture of Exclusion: After 1948

The war did not settle the conflict; it institutionalized it.

The Palestinians who remained inside the new State of Israel became citizens but lived under military rule until 1966. They were subject to curfews, movement restrictions, land confiscation, and discriminatory administrative detention. The Palestinians who fled to Gaza, the West Bank (then annexed by Jordan), Lebanon, Syria, and Jordan became refugees. Their descendants now number in the millions.

Israel's **Law of Return (1950)** grants Jews anywhere in the world the right to immigrate to Israel and gain citizenship. Palestinian refugees who hold title to property inside Israel—property that in many cases was seized or transferred to the state—are denied the same right.

This double standard is the legal core of the conflict. It says, in effect, that a Jew from any country has a claim to the land that a Palestinian refugee from Jaffa or Haifa does not.

After 1967, Israel occupied the West Bank, East Jerusalem, Gaza, and the Golan Heights. The occupation brought settlements, settlement roads, military courts, expropriation orders, house demolitions, and the separation wall. Each of these policies extends the demographic and territorial logic that began under the British Mandate: the expansion of Jewish control over land and the confinement or displacement of Palestinians.

## VIII. Sources, Interpretations, and the Weight of the Record

This history is contested. Israeli nationalist historiography long maintained that Palestinians left in 1948 at the urging of Arab leaders. That claim has been largely abandoned by serious Israeli historians. **Benny Morris**, using Israeli military archives, documented numerous cases of expulsion by Israeli forces, though he maintains that the refugee outcome was primarily a consequence of war rather than a premeditated master plan. **Ilan Pappé** has argued instead that ethnic cleansing was central to Zionist planning and execution. **Rashid Khalidi** has emphasized the weakness of Palestinian national organization, decapitated by the British during the 1936–39 revolt and outmatched diplomatically at the U.N.

The factual baseline is not seriously disputed among mainstream scholars:

- Britain made contradictory wartime promises.
- The Mandate privileged Jewish immigration and state-building over Arab self-determination.
- Jews owned a small fraction of the land but were granted the majority of the territory under the partition plan.
- Palestinians were not represented in the U.N. deliberations.
- Approximately 750,000 Palestinians became refugees.
- Israel ended the war with 78% of Mandatory Palestine.

Beyond these facts lie competing moral and political frameworks: restoration of an ancient Jewish homeland, versus settler-colonial dispossession; self-determination, versus denial of Palestinian sovereignty. The purpose of recounting the history is not to litigate identity but to recognize the material process through which one national project was advanced at the expense of another.

## IX. Conclusion

The partition of Palestine was not a fair two-state solution. It was an international decision imposed on a colonized majority by foreign powers, many of which had already decided that a Jewish national home was the preferred outcome. The British laid the legal and demographic foundation. The U.N. provided international legitimacy. Zionist militias and then the Israeli army completed the conquest and the expulsion.

The violence was present from the beginning: the evictions of tenant farmers, the suppression of the Arab revolt, the bombing of the King David Hotel, the massacres of 1948, the blocking of refugee return. It did not emerge later as a failure of diplomacy. It was built into the structure of the project.

Understanding this is not an endorsement of any particular modern policy. It is a precondition for understanding why the conflict persists: because the foundation has never been repaired.
